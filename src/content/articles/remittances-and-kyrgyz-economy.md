Remittances from Kyrgyz citizens working abroad — overwhelmingly in Russia — are among the largest in the world relative to the size of the economy. They pay for food, school fees, weddings and houses. They also make the country unusually exposed to shocks it cannot control: exchange-rate swings, changes to migration rules, and economic downturns in host countries.

## The consumption trap

Most remittance income is spent on consumption and housing rather than invested in productive businesses. That is rational for households — but at the national level it means that a large inflow of money has not translated into a broad base of firms and jobs.

## Three policy levers

**1. Make saving and investing easier than spending.** Low-cost transfer channels, diaspora savings products and simple ways for families to co-invest in local businesses can shift a share of remittances toward productive uses.

**2. Skills that travel both ways.** Pre-departure training improves migrants’ earnings abroad; recognition of skills gained abroad helps returnees start businesses at home.

**3. Diversify destinations and sectors.** Agreements opening labour markets in other countries reduce dependence on a single host economy.

## The demographic stakes

Kyrgyzstan has a young population. If the domestic economy cannot absorb new entrants to the labour market, migration will remain the default choice for the ambitious. Remittances are a lifeline — but a lifeline is not a strategy.
