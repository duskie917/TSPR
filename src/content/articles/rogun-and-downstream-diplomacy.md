Few infrastructure projects in Central Asia carry as much political weight as the **Rogun hydropower plant** on the Vakhsh river. For Dushanbe, it promises energy independence and export revenue. For downstream states, it once raised fears about water for irrigated agriculture.

## From confrontation to coordination

During the 2000s and early 2010s, upstream–downstream disputes over water were among the region’s sharpest bilateral tensions. That climate has changed. Since 2016, a broader thaw in regional relations has made it possible to discuss reservoir operations, electricity trade and joint projects in technical rather than existential terms.

## Why water and power must be negotiated together

The underlying bargain in the Amu Darya and Syr Darya basins is straightforward to describe and hard to implement:

- Upstream states want to store water in summer and release it in winter, when they need electricity.
- Downstream states need water in summer for irrigation.

The Soviet-era solution — downstream fuel and power sent upstream in winter in exchange for summer water releases — collapsed with the USSR. Rebuilding a version of it, with market-based electricity trade and transparent data on flows, is the most promising path.

## What to watch

- **Data sharing.** Real-time hydrological data published by all basin states would reduce suspicion more than any summit communiqué.
- **Electricity trade.** A functioning regional power market makes it rational for upstream reservoirs to be operated with downstream needs in mind.
- **Climate stress.** Glacier retreat in the Pamir and Tian Shan mountains will change the timing of flows. Agreements must be built to adapt.

Rogun will not be the last large dam in the region. How it is operated may set the template for all the others.
