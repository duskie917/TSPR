For most of the past three decades, the fastest way to move goods overland between China and Europe ran north, through Kazakhstan and Russia. Since 2022, sanctions, insurance costs and reputational risk have pushed shippers to look south and west — across Kazakhstan to the Caspian Sea, then through Azerbaijan, Georgia and Türkiye. The Trans-Caspian International Transport Route, better known as the **Middle Corridor**, suddenly has customers.

## Demand is not the bottleneck

Volumes on the route have grown quickly from a low base, but the corridor remains a chain of separate systems: rail gauges change, ferries across the Caspian run on uncertain schedules, and each border adds paperwork. A container that should take two weeks can take far longer.

The constraint, in other words, is not interest from China or the EU. It is coordination among the transit states themselves.

## Three things that would make the difference

1. **A single through-tariff and digital paperwork.** Shippers want one price and one set of documents from Xinjiang to the Black Sea. Harmonised e-customs would do more than any new mega-project.
2. **Port capacity at both ends of the Caspian.** Ferry and port capacity at Aktau, Kuryk and Baku determines the throughput of the entire route.
3. **Predictability.** Logistics firms commit to a route when they can promise customers delivery windows. That requires published schedules and transparent dispute resolution.

## Leverage, carefully used

For Astana, the corridor is more than a revenue stream. It is a hedge — a way to diversify dependence on a single northern route and to deepen ties with Europe without breaking with anyone. That is classic multi-vector policy.

But leverage evaporates if the route under-performs. The window opened by geopolitics will not stay open forever; if the Middle Corridor cannot offer reliability, cargo will drift back to cheaper or faster alternatives as soon as conditions allow.

> The test for the Middle Corridor is not whether it can attract headlines, but whether it can attract repeat customers.

The good news is that most of the fixes are within the region’s control — and cheaper than the railways and ports that often dominate the announcements.
